The equation starts with the two-tie pattern but replaces one passive square by a sum:
\[
F=F_0+X_0^2\cdot X_1^2\cdot(Y_0^2+Y_2^2)
+X_2^2\cdot Y_1^2\cdot Y_2^2.
\]
The same variable \(Y_2\) now participates in both couplings. Thus the two visible ternary pieces no longer occupy disjoint sets of variables, even though the equation still has substantial structure.

The target is
\[
A=X_0\cdot X_1\cdot X_2\cdot Y_1\cdot
(4\cdot X_0^2+5\cdot X_1^2).
\]
The remaining linear combination distinguishes two target directions while keeping their shared parity character fixed.

The relevant question is whether a contact construction treats \(Y_0^2+Y_2^2\) as one passive quadratic quantity or needs it to be the square of a single coordinate. Those are different hypotheses. The former would allow a substantially larger family of couplings; the latter could explain an isolated formula without providing a useful extension. The term involving \(X_2,Y_1,Y_2\) supplies a concrete test of this distinction.

The quadratic \(Y_0^2+Y_2^2\) has rank two: it cannot become a single square under an invertible change of its two variables. The proposed extension is therefore substantive even over an algebraically closed coefficient field.

This target was also useful in numerical searches for rational fields: a complicated high-degree representation can obscure a much smaller underlying denominator. The instance therefore asks both for structural understanding of passive coefficients and for recognition of redundant presentation complexity. It does not impose a degree bound on the answer.
