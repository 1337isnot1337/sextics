This sextic retains a ternary active block but contains odd powers of individual coordinates, notably \(Y_1^3\cdot Y_2^3\). It is globally \(Y\)-even without being a cubic in all six coordinate squares. That makes it a useful test of whether contact methods genuinely extend beyond the especially symmetric coordinatewise-even setting.

There is nevertheless a special presentation. With
\[
R=Y_2^3+\frac12\cdot X_2^2\cdot Y_2,
\]
the equation can be organized as \(X_1^6+(Y_1^3+R)^2+G(X_0,X_2,Y_0,Y_2)\). Completing the square exposes a potential contact structure while leaving a nontrivial remainder. The target \(X_1^2\cdot X_2\cdot Y_1^2\cdot Y_2\) links the square's active variables to two further coordinates.

The exponents are meaningful: differentiating the two cubic expressions naturally produces square factors, whereas \(X_2\cdot Y_2\) supplies the remaining degree and odd parity. The question is whether those ingredients can be assembled into a rational field without losing the prescribed target or introducing an uncancelled divergence term. Merely writing \(F\) as a sum containing a square does not answer that question.

This instance arose in the study of exceptional target directions that did not fit the earlier binary templates. It is a controlled setting for testing Nambu fields, whose first integrals can preserve the completed-square structure, and for comparing constructions over an extension field with formulas defined over \(\mathbb Q\). [Chapter 2](#technical-information/explicit-constructions) develops these tools. The word “holdout” in its stable identifier refers to that earlier local test; it is a different variety from [the central holdout](#case/holdout).
