This example separates knowing that a certificate exists from finding a usable one. The sextic has substantial interaction among all six coordinates, but retains the single reflection Y_2\mapsto-Y_2. The target A=X_0^3\cdot Y_2^3 is odd under that reflection.

Nonconstructive existence is known, by the reflection theorem in Chapter 3, §3.3, combined with the certificate equivalence in Chapter 1. In the quotient coordinate u=Y_2^2, the equation defines a smooth degree-six hypersurface in \mathbb P(2,1,1,1,1,1). Its anticanonical class is \mathcal O(1), so it is Fano. The sign of the residue volume cancels the odd sign of A, placing the residue in the quotient's cohomology. A covering family of rational curves supplies divisor support, and hence a certificate.

The missing object is an explicit denominator and numerator tuple for this exact target. The book explains how the existence argument can, in principle, be made into a finite algebraic search: find a free rational curve of weighted degree at most five, extract a covering family, and compute a supporting divisor from its boundary and branch loci. That is a constructive route in principle, not an evaluated polynomial witness for this instance.

Why not use the easier two-reflection construction? There is no individual X_i reflection here. Nor is the equation a disguised linear sum of independent blocks: its rational Hessian symmetrizer space consists only of scalar matrices. Thus the ternary-split and binary-block formulas do not apply after a linear change of coordinates.

A useful first contribution would be a concrete free curve on the Fano quotient with a verified deformation-rank calculation. Even if eliminating its support is too expensive initially, this would replace a major existence input with explicit geometry. A direct rational-field construction would be equally valuable.
