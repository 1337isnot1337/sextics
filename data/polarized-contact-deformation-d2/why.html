Take the coupled polarized-contact instance as a starting point, and add

101000303\cdot(X_2^2-Y_2^2)\cdot Y_1^4

to its sextic. The perturbation respects the global involution but does not simply replace a passive block by another polynomial in untouched variables. It changes the equation along the contact geometry that organized the original construction.

The target changes at the same time, by the specified odd sextic correction in the instance record. This is intentional. Asking whether an old target stays solvable under an arbitrary deformation is generally much harder than asking whether a class can be transported according to a controlled rule. Keeping both F and A explicit makes the distinction visible.

The quadratic factor X_2^2-Y_2^2 is the product of the two polarized contact coordinates. Multiples of that factor form a natural space of perturbations to investigate. Their effect on a candidate field can be analyzed by the product rule, but the resulting correction must still have the proper degree and odd parity.

The coefficient is 101\cdot1000003, preserving two useful finite-field comparisons with the starting example. Those comparisons provide a way to control the specialization without appealing to a vague assertion that a large perturbation is “close” to the original equation. A separate Hessian calculation still excludes a hidden linear block decomposition.

This entry tests a deformation principle rather than an unrelated new isolated formula. Its value is to identify a family of changes under which mathematical information can be carried forward, while being explicit about the fact that the target moves. The coupled quartic-cofactor example gives a smaller comparison with the same distinction. Neither instance asserts persistence of every target under arbitrary deformations.
