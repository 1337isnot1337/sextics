The useful decomposition here crosses the notation's X/Y boundary:

F=P(X_0,X_1,Y_0)+R(X_2,Y_1,Y_2).

Each three-dimensional summand is preserved by the global sign involution, but their negative eigenspace dimensions are one and two. This differs from the usual block-sign example in which all three Y coordinates form one summand. The target A=X_0^4\cdot X_1\cdot Y_1 has block degrees (5,1).

Nonconstructive existence is known, by “Involution-stable ternary decompositions” in Chapter 3A, §3A.3. To see which part of that theorem matters, let r negate Y_0, let s negate X_2, and let \rho negate the entire second ternary block. The prescribed involution is \tau=\rho\cdot r\cdot s. Our numerator is even under both individual reflections, so its residue is anti-invariant under each. It lies instead in the \rho-invariant sector, which is supported by the split theorem and its elliptic contraction.

Thus simply noticing two coordinate reflections is not enough: they support the wrong residue characters for this target. Nor does the immediate split Euler identity apply, since neither block degree is three.

An explicit construction would demonstrate that the crossed-block theorem is computationally useful beyond the already accessible reflection sectors. One route is to perform the elliptic-companion construction on the two displayed ternary curves, retaining the involution characters throughout the trace. Another is to search for a much smaller contact divisor suggested by those same block variables. No particular degree bound on the denominator is part of the challenge.

The Hessian symmetrizer space has dimension two, generated by the identity and the displayed block projector. Thus this is the only linear splitting, up to exchanging blocks: the crossed decomposition cannot be replaced by a many-block Fermat presentation. That makes it a focused test of the geometric theorem beyond the simpler formulas.
