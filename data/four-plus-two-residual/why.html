This example preserves an additive decomposition, but changes the dimensions of the blocks:

F=H(X_0,X_1,Y_0,Y_1)+B(X_2,Y_2).

The four-variable core is coupled and the binary sextic is separately even. The target A=X_0^3\cdot Y_0^3 lives entirely in the core. It is even in each binary coordinate, so its residue is anti-invariant under both of the visible coordinate reflections. The reflection theorem does not serve this sector.

This is a boundary of the ternary-split and binary-block methods. The all-target theorem in Chapter 3A is a 3+3 theorem: its two cyclic-cover surfaces have elliptic Fano companions whose supported pieces can be contracted. A 4+2 decomposition produces different-dimensional covering varieties. For example, the natural triple cover of the four-variable core,

T^3=H(X_0,X_1,Y_0,Y_1)\subset\mathbb P(1,1,1,1,2),

has trivial canonical class by weighted adjunction, rather than being Fano. Merely carrying over the rational-curve argument therefore does not establish support. The finite contact-cover formulas for three binary blocks in Chapter 2a likewise require a decomposition of the core that is not present here.

The split remains useful structure: its binary factor has a small, explicit branch set, and a joining construction can separate contributions from that factor and the core. It may be possible to identify the target's relevant character on the resulting cover and construct a correspondence or contact divisor there. A solution should explain how that step replaces the missing ternary elliptic contraction.

The elementary Euler construction also misses this target. For the four-variable block it requires target degree two, and for the binary block degree four; the actual degrees are six and zero.

The Hessian symmetrizer space is generated by the identity and the 4+2 projector. No linear change turns this into a 3+3 split or three binary blocks. No all-target existence theorem for this configuration is asserted here.
