This equation begins with a polarized split sextic but adds a coupling between its two ternary blocks. In U=X+Y, V=X-Y, it is

F=f(U)+f(V)+1000003\cdot r(U)\cdot r(V), \qquad f=r^2+z_2\cdot Q,

where r is cubic and Q is quintic. The involution exchanges U,V, so the coupling respects the required global parity.

The underlying plane geometry is a square contact along z_2=0. On the double-plane K3 surface w^2=f, that line lifts into two components distinguished by w=\pm r. It supplies a concrete geometric feature to investigate in the part of polarized split cohomology that is not automatically handled by the block-sign theorem. Chapter 3A explains why the block swap leaves a different problem.

The target is the antisymmetric difference g(U)-g(V) attached to this contact presentation. The question is whether the same geometric direction can survive an actual coupling, rather than merely a change of linear coordinates. Here exact coefficient checks exclude every nontrivial linear direct-sum decomposition of the coupled sextic.

The coefficient 1000003 was chosen to make reduction to the uncoupled equation available at a prime where smoothness can be checked. Its large real magnitude is not the reason the example is interesting. The structural issue is that the coupling is built from the same cubic quantities that organize the contact geometry.

This makes the entry a controlled bridge from special split constructions to genuinely coupled equations. It asks which couplings preserve a target construction and which require new ideas. The moving-target deformation then relaxes the requirement that the target itself remain unchanged.
