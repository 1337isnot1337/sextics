This entry keeps the Fermat hypersurface and changes the pole parameter to \(q=2\). The target
\[
A=X_0^3\cdot X_1^2\cdot X_2^2\cdot Y_0^3\cdot Y_1^2
\]
has degree twelve, and its differential form has a third-order pole along \(F=0\). The key equation must now be divisible by \(F^3\), so checking only a degree-six analogue or the leading Jacobian relation would not settle the instance.

The exponents have been chosen to make a useful homogeneity comparison possible. The degree in \(X_1,X_2\) is four, and those are two coordinates. Their sum is six, the degree of the defining equation. This is the balance used by the subset-Euler construction in [Chapter 2](#technical-information/explicit-constructions). The remaining eight degrees of the target lie in variables that can be passive for that selected motion.

The higher pole makes the distinction between a fixed identity and a reusable construction particularly clear. A field that happens to work at \(q=1\) need not survive changing the coefficient of \(dF(v)\). A homogeneity argument that tracks \(q\) explicitly can explain why an entire sequence of pole orders behaves uniformly.

This is a deliberately tractable control for higher-pole computations. The diagonal all-target theorem supplies a constructive setting in which the change from \(q=1\) to \(q=2\) can be understood without additional coupling geometry. It helps distinguish errors in homogeneity, the factor of \(q\), or the order of divisibility from a genuine difficulty in choosing a support.

[The open holdout at \(q=2\)](#case/holdout-q2) uses the same target on a coupled sextic. That comparison isolates what the mixed terms destroy. [The one-block \(q=2\) bundle](#case/block-q2) offers an intermediate step, retaining enough structure to study a nontrivial target compatibility relation.
