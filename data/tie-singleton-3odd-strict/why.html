The equation has one ternary tie, X_0^2\cdot X_1^2\cdot Y_1^2, but the target

A=X_0^3\cdot Y_0\cdot Y_1\cdot Y_2

involves all three Y coordinates. Only Y_1 belongs to the tied block; Y_0 and Y_2 remain outside it. This arrangement asks how much information about a target is carried by its active factors and how much can be supplied by passive multipliers.

The three odd factors distinguish this from the single-odd-coordinate targets on the same equation. A construction confined to the tied block would naturally see only the factor in Y_1; the other two must enter as compatible coefficients. This makes the example a clean test of how target spaces grow when independent passive factors are available.

The comparison with the off-block target is particularly informative. There the passive part includes Y_0^2; here it becomes Y_0\cdot Y_2. If a method truly leaves both variables untouched, such a replacement should preserve its essential structure. If it secretly differentiates one of them, the divergence equation will detect the difference.

The instance was part of a systematic effort to vary the location of odd exponents on single-tie sextics. It tests the passage from a certificate for one target direction to a controlled space of directions, while retaining a single monomial as the requested answer. Chapter 2 explains why first integrals and passive factors matter. The aim is to identify the precise hypothesis that permits such substitutions, rather than infer a whole target family from one successful numerical calculation.
