Replace the passive square in a single-tie construction by the genuine binary quadratic
\[
R=\frac{13}{17}\cdot Y_0^2+\frac59\cdot Y_0\cdot Y_2
+\frac7{11}\cdot Y_2^2.
\]
The active part of the sextic is \(X_0^6+X_1^6+X_0^2\cdot X_1^2\cdot R\), while another tie couples \(X_2,Y_1,Y_2\). The cross term \(Y_0\cdot Y_2\) removes some individual sign symmetries but preserves the global involution.

The requested target is \(X_0^3\cdot X_1\cdot X_2\cdot Y_1\). Its last two factors come from outside the active pair \(X_0,X_1\). This makes the example a precise test of passive dependence: can the active contact geometry treat an arbitrary quadratic in untouched variables as a coefficient, even when those variables also occur in another coupled part of \(F\)?

The distinction matters for extending a construction. A formula proved only when \(R\) is a single square does not automatically survive this substitution. Conversely, if its actual hypothesis is that the field preserves \(R\), the mixed quadratic may be no harder than the original square. The target and the second tie make those alternatives directly checkable.
